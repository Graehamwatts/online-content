The Rent-It-Out Math, Honestly

Your agent's suggestion to rent is not crazy, so here is the math with nothing hidden. A 1,580 sq ft 2/2.5 gated townhome in East Palo Alto realistically rents for $3,800 to $4,500 a month. Against that:

Monthly line itemEstimate
Gross rent$3,800 to $4,500
HOA dues (gated community, exterior, common area)roughly -$500 to -$600
Property tax (on your assessed basis)roughly -$870
Insurance, maintenance reserve, vacancy and turnover allowanceroughly -$350 to -$500
Net before any mortgageroughly +$2,100 to +$2,300

That is roughly a 3% gross yield on your equity at today's value, before counting your mortgage payment if you carry one, and before the work of being a landlord in East Palo Alto, which has some of the strongest tenant protections in the state (rent stabilization, just-cause eviction, relocation assistance). Getting a tenant out later to sell is a real cost and a real timeline.

The tax clock is the part nobody mentions. You have lived in the home, so up to $250,000 of gain ($500,000 married) is tax-free under the Section 121 exclusion if you sell while you still qualify: you need 2 of the last 5 years as your primary residence. Rent the home past the three-year mark and the exclusion evaporates. On a sale around $920,000 against your $826,000 basis, the roughly $80,000 to $105,000 gain (selling costs considered) is likely entirely tax-free today. As a rental sold in year four or beyond, that gain becomes taxable, plus depreciation recapture. Renting does not just delay the sale; past three years it changes the tax character of every dollar of gain, including the appreciation you already earned.
Why the Listing Has Not Sold. And Why That Is Fixable

Here is the part that should reframe the whole conversation: your floor plan went into contract at $924,888 in 20 days, this spring, in this complex. Your unit has sat 45+ days at $907,880, a LOWER price. When the identical product sells faster at a higher price, the variable is not the market and it is not the price. It is presentation, marketing reach, or both.

Before concluding the home "won't sell and should be rented," the checklist looks like: professional photos that lead with the size advantage (you are the largest unit format in the complex and the largest attached home in the city), staging or decluttering to compete with how the $960K and $924,888 units showed, broad MLS syndication and open-house cadence, and a relaunch strategy if days-on-market has gone stale (a refreshed listing at $899,000 to $925,000 with proper presentation would likely generate the competition the sister unit saw). The in-complex evidence supports a sale at $905,000 to $930,000. Renting because the current listing stalled would mean accepting a 3% yield and a ticking tax clock to avoid a presentation problem that costs a few thousand dollars to fix.
