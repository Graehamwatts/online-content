THE MARKET STORY

This property is two days from closing as of today, but the path to get here was anything but smooth, and that path is the most useful piece of information for what to do next. Listed January 8 at $1,098,000, it went into contract in just 14 days. Then on February 18 the deal fell apart and it went back to the market. It took roughly three more weeks of active marketing and a second buyer to get re-contracted on March 13, with the close scheduled for April 29.

A 14-day contract on the first round tells me the price was reasonable enough to attract real buyers. A fall-out three weeks later tells me something material came up during contingencies — almost always inspections, appraisal, or both. With this property, the listing remarks themselves flag it: "buyers and/or buyers' agent should verify permits, building square footage and lot size." That language paired with a fully self-contained rear unit is a tell. The rear unit is almost certainly unpermitted, which means the appraiser cannot give it dollar-for-dollar value, the lender will only finance against the main 1,100 sqft house, and any buyer financing through a conforming loan can run into a low appraisal.

Here is what the East Palo Alto market is actually doing right now. According to the MLSListings stats panel, single-family homes in EPA closed 4 sales in March 2026 at a median of $1,218,500 in 12 median days on market, with sellers receiving 101% of list, at $802 per square foot. February ran 8 sales at $1,195,000 median, 15 DOM, 100% list-to-sale, $855 per square foot. The Aculist 94303 ZIP report (90-day rolling) shows median sale of $1.11M and $792 per square foot across 20 closed sales. Translation: the market is balanced, transacting near asking in two-to-three weeks, and the typical EPA single-family home is trading in the $790–$855 per square foot range.

Now compare to the subject's pricing. The list price of $1,098,000 on 1,100 sqft is $998 per square foot — roughly 17–25% above the EPA price-per-foot average. That premium is justifiable if (and only if) the rear unit is treated as appraisable bonus living space. Without a permit, the appraiser ignores it, and the math reverts back to the main house alone — which on a comparable basis (3 bed / 1 bath / similar size / similar age) is selling closer to $925,000–$1,125,000. The most directly comparable sale, 2544 Illinois Street (a 3/1 / 970 sqft / 6,292 lot / 73 years / 24 DOM), closed at $1,125,000 — essentially at the same dollar level, but with no comparable bonus unit, suggesting that property was a fully renovated showpiece commanding premium per-foot pricing.

The two same-street comps are the most uncomfortable data point for the seller. 2352 Menalto closed at $900,000 (2/1 / 1,010 sqft / 6,480 lot / 30 DOM) and 2210 Menalto closed at $850,000 (2/1 / 920 sqft / 5,000 lot / 23 DOM). Both are smaller in bedroom count, but they sit on the same block and tell the appraiser what Menalto Avenue itself supports. They anchor any future appraisal review, and they do not flatter $1,098,000.

Putting it together: the current pending price is defensible, but only because a buyer chose to value the bonus unit subjectively. If this deal closes Wednesday, the seller wins and that's the end of the conversation. If it does not close — if there is a second appraisal issue, a financing collapse, or a buyer remorse cancellation — the relisting strategy needs to change. That is what the rest of this analysis is built to address.

PRICING STRATEGY ANALYSIS

STRATEGY 1 — PRICE BELOW MARKET

$1,025,000 – $1,050,000 · ~$932 – $955 / SqFt

Comps that played this strategy: 2509 Illinois Street (3/1 / 1,030 sqft / sold $955k in 19 DOM), 1007 Newbridge Street ($960k in 28 DOM), 120 Gardenia Way ($930k / 27 DOM). All cleared in under 30 days at 100% of asking.

For 2247 Menalto, a price below $1,050k pulls the listing back to a "no-appraisal-risk" zone — even ignoring the bonus unit entirely, the comps support this number on the main 1,100 sqft house. This is the strategy I'd recommend if a cash buyer is unlikely and the seller wants speed and certainty.

Pros

Multiple offers likely. Faster close. Appraisal-proof. No seller concession negotiation.

Risks

Leaves money on the table if a buyer would have valued the bonus unit. Requires permit conversation upfront.

STRATEGY 2 — PRICE AT MARKET (current)

$1,075,000 – $1,100,000 · ~$977 – $1,000 / SqFt

This is exactly where the current pending price sits. Comps that supported this band: 2544 Illinois Street ($1,125k / 24 DOM, true 3/1 comp), 2055 Oakwood Drive ($1,035k / 8 DOM), 151 Verbena Drive (pending at $1,068k / 7 DOM).

The current $1,098,000 list is defensible if a buyer subjectively values the rear unit. The data shows it took 33 active days plus one fall-out to find that buyer — meaning the price-to-product fit is right at the edge. If this deal closes Wednesday, this strategy works. If it falls out, this same strategy on a relisting will likely produce another long DOM and another financing risk.

Pros

Captures the bonus-unit premium when a buyer values it. Matches what the market just bore.

Risks

Appraisal sensitivity. Slower DOM. Fall-out risk if buyer financing tightens.

STRATEGY 3 — PRICE ABOVE MARKET (only with permit fix)

$1,150,000 – $1,225,000 · ~$1,045 – $1,113 / SqFt

Comps in this band: 1523 Ursula Way ($1,220k / 50 DOM), 2550 Annapolis Street ($1,200k / 11 DOM), 1371 Camellia Drive (pending $1,248k / 19 DOM). All are 3 bed / 2 bath, fully updated. The Menalto subject is 3 bed / 1 bath with an unpermitted bonus unit — it cannot reach this band as-is.

This range becomes available only if the rear unit is properly permitted (or if a permit-in-progress with an estimated cost is provided to buyers as a credit). Permitting an ADU in EPA typically takes 4–8 months and runs $15,000–$45,000 depending on existing condition. The math: spend $25k to gain $75–125k in sale price upside.

Pros

Highest seller proceeds. Bonus unit is appraisable. Wider buyer pool (including FHA/conventional with second-unit factor).

Risks

4–8 month permit timeline. Holding cost. No guarantee the city approves the existing structure.

SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

1. Unpermitted Rear Unit (the central issue)

The listing's own remarks instruct buyers to "verify permits, building square footage, and lot size." Combined with a fully self-contained rear unit (kitchen, bath, separate entrance), this almost certainly indicates the unit is unpermitted. The appraiser will assign zero square-foot value to an unpermitted addition. This is the single biggest pricing variable, the most likely cause of the February fall-out, and the determining factor on which of the three pricing strategies above is appropriate.

2. Buyer Fall-Out History on Public Record

The MLS history is permanent and transparent: the property went from Active → Active Under Contract on January 22, back to Active on February 18, then Active Under Contract again March 13. Any future buyer's agent will pull this and treat it as a yellow flag. If the current pending closes, this becomes a footnote. If it falls out a second time, the property is materially harder to relist — buyers will assume there is a real defect and price accordingly. That is the strongest argument for resolving the permit question before any future relisting.

3. Seller Equity Context — The 2021 Frenzy Purchase

Owner Cheng Chiou-Pying purchased December 3, 2021 for $1,020,000 (originally listed at $789,000 — paid $231k over asking in the late-2021 frenzy). Mortgage of $800,000 with PNC Bank. At the current pending price of $1,098,000, after 6% commission, transfer tax, and standard sell-side closing costs, the seller's net is roughly $1,020,000 — essentially break-even before factoring 4.5 years of carrying cost and improvements. This explains the seller's pricing reluctance and is useful context for negotiation if any concession discussion arises.

4. Lot Size & Development Upside

6,500 sqft lot under R10006 zoning supports an ADU (which is what the rear unit appears to attempt). California's SB 9 and EPA's local ADU ordinance permit substantial second-unit development on lots this size. For a buyer who wants to permit the existing structure as a junior ADU and add a primary ADU, the long-term value upside is meaningful — that's a marketing angle the current listing has not used.

5. School District & Buyer Pool

Ravenswood Elementary / Ravenswood Middle / Sequoia Union High. Sequoia Union is the strongest of the three for resale appeal. Buyer pool is split between investors (the larger share at this price point in EPA), first-time buyers using FHA, and Stanford/Meta employee buyers cross-shopping with Belle Haven and Menlo Park. The unpermitted unit narrows the FHA pool meaningfully.

6. No A/C, No Garage, Vacant

Property is vacant and staged (a positive — easy to show), but lacks a garage (driveway parking only) and has no air conditioning. These are small individual items but compound when buyers are comparing to the renovated 3/2 comp set in Tier 3. They reinforce why the property belongs in the Conservative-to-Competitive band, not the Stretch band, without the permit fix.
