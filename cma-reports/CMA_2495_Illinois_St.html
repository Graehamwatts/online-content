SB9: Could You Actually Split This Lot?

You asked about this directly, so here is the real mechanics of it, not just the headline.

California's SB9 (in effect since January 2022) lets an owner of a single-family-zoned lot in an urbanized area split it into two legal parcels through a ministerial process, meaning the city has to approve or deny it within 60 days and cannot hold a public hearing or apply subjective design review to block it. Here is how the specific rules land on your 6,952 sq ft corner lot:

Minimum parcel size1,200 sq ft statewide floor, and neither resulting parcel can be smaller than 40% of the original lot. On your lot that means each new parcel must be at least 2,781 sq ft. You clear this easily, even a roughly even 3,476 / 3,476 sq ft split leaves both parcels well above the minimum.
Units per parcelUp to 2 residential units on each resulting parcel. In practice this maps cleanly onto what you already have: the front house becomes its own parcel (with room to add a future ADU later if you want), and the back unit, which already has its own address and kitchen, becomes the primary dwelling on its own new parcel.
Access / frontageBeing a corner lot is a real advantage here. A corner lot commonly already fronts two streets, which typically makes it much simpler and cheaper to give both resulting parcels independent street access, rather than needing to carve a shared driveway or flag-lot easement the way an interior lot often does. Worth confirming the exact frontage with a surveyor, but this is the standout positive in your specific situation.
Owner-occupancy requirementWhoever applies for the lot split must sign an affidavit committing to occupy one of the housing units on one of the resulting parcels as their principal residence for a minimum of 3 years from approval. Since you already live there, this is satisfiable, but it directly affects your timeline if the plan is to sell right away. See the note below.
Other restrictionsNeither resulting unit can be rented short-term (under 30 days). The law also restricts demolishing more than a portion of an existing structure's exterior walls in certain tenant-protection circumstances, not a factor here since there is no rental/tenant history on file.
Local standardsEast Palo Alto can still apply its own objective standards, setbacks, height limits, parking, but cannot use discretionary review to reject a qualifying application. The exact local standards should be confirmed with the city planning department before filing.
The head start you already have: a from-scratch SB9 project usually has to build out separate utilities, addressing, and a functionally independent second unit before it even gets to the survey and mapping stage. You are past most of that: the back unit already has its own address and its own kitchen, and the county already classifies the property as "Single Family W/ Adu." That materially lowers both the cost and the risk of this specific split compared to a typical case.
The part that needs a real conversation before you file anything: the 3-year occupancy clock. If you apply for the split yourself, the law expects you to live in one of the two units for 3 years after approval before you're free to sell without restriction tied to that requirement. If the plan is to sell soon, there are two honest paths: (1) apply and hold for the 3 years, then sell one or both with a clean title history, or (2) complete the ministerial split and sell the resulting parcels to a buyer who takes on the process from there, which can mean a lower price since you're transferring un-built-out lots rather than finished, marketable homes. This is exactly the kind of question to run past a real estate attorney or the city planner before committing either way, and I'm happy to sit in on that conversation.
