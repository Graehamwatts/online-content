What the Market Is Doing

East Palo Alto's single-family median is running around $1,090,000, up modestly from last year, at roughly $800 per square foot. Well-priced homes are moving fast right now, around 12 days on market at about 104% of asking, but pricing is what drives that speed. Over the last three to five years EPA price per square foot has been essentially flat, so this is a steady market, not one that supports a reach.

The subject's edge is size. At 2,050 square feet it is meaningfully larger than the 1,100 to 1,250 square foot homes that make up most of the area and set that $800 median. That size premium is real and it lifts the number above a typical EPA sale. What it does not do is move the home into a different market tier. Condition is the other half of the story: an original 1956 home needing about $100,000 of work prices off as-is comps, not renovated ones.

Renovating lifts the price by roughly $200,000 but costs about $100,000 plus carrying time and execution risk, so the net gain is modest, on the order of $100,000 before selling costs. For a long-held or estate seller who wants a clean, fast exit, listing as-is at a sharp number around $1.1M is usually the better path: it draws the contractor and investor pool immediately and avoids tying up cash in a remodel. The renovate-first path only makes sense if the seller has the time, capital, and appetite for the project.

Why $1.8M to $2M Does Not Pencil

The price expectation is worth walking through, because it shapes how we position the home. A $1.8M to $2M target is roughly $600,000 to $900,000 above what the East Palo Alto sales support for a home like this, and here is the data behind that.

  • The citywide EPA median is about $1.09M, at roughly $800 per square foot, and that figure has been flat for several years.
  • Even fully renovated and newer-built 4-bedroom homes (Wilks, Ralmar) top out around $1.3M to $1.43M.
  • The single highest EPA single-family sale in the last twelve months was 206 Donohoe Street at $1,925,000. That was a 3-bed on a 9,133 square foot lot, nearly double this lot, marketed as a two-unit property (206 and 206A) with a legal second unit. It sold high because of the oversized lot and the income unit, not the finishes.

So the path to the high $1M range and above in EPA runs through lot size and extra units, not through cosmetics on a standard 5,500 square foot lot. Pricing 1419 Kavanaugh at $1.8M would draw few showings, accumulate days on market, and most likely sell for less than a sharply priced launch near $1.15M would have. The fastest route to the strongest number is to list it at a price the comps defend and let the size advantage and the fast spring market create competition.

Diligence Before Listing
Two items to confirm, both of which can move the number:
  • Permitted square footage. The 2,050 sq ft figure is the single biggest value lever here. If part of it is unpermitted, the defensible value drops toward the smaller-home comps. Pull the permit history and confirm before we set the list price.
  • Vesting and authority to sell. The assessor record shows the Versie L. Williams Trust with a very low Prop 13 basis, which points to a long-held or estate situation. Confirm the trustee or executor has authority and whether a probate process applies, since that affects timeline and terms.

List as-is around $1,095,000 to $1,150,000 to draw the contractor, investor, and renovate-it-yourself owner-occupant pool from day one.
