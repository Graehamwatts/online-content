01. Why this matters

The agent's real problem

Real estate agents are drowning in tools and starving for outcomes. A typical top-25% agent today is paying for a CRM, an MLS subscription, a transaction management platform, a postcard print service, an ad agency, a video editor, a virtual assistant, a coaching subscription, and three or four social schedulers. None of these tools talk to each other. None of them tell the agent what to do next. Every Monday, the agent stares at a screen full of dashboards and still has no idea which lead matters most, which content piece deserves spend, or which household in their farm is closest to selling.

PropertyIQ replaces the dashboard problem with a command center. Instead of seven tools and zero answers, the agent opens one screen that tells them: here is what your market did this week, here is what we recommend you post, here is the household most likely to call you back, here is what your ads earned you. The complexity is still there — it has to be, because the work is genuinely complex — but the surface is calm. The depth is one click away when the agent wants it.

The specific problems the site has to make agents feel

When a real estate agent reads the landing page, they should recognize themselves in the first three lines. These are the friction points to put on the page — paraphrase, don't quote verbatim:

Seven tools. Zero answers. CRM, MLS, video editor, ad platform, postcard service, scheduler, coaching app — all open, all costing money, none of them talking. Monday morning the agent has four dashboards in four tabs and no idea what to do first.

The CRM is a graveyard. Forty leads sitting in the pipeline. The agent touched two of them this week. The other thirty-eight have forgotten the agent exists.

Content takes an afternoon and produces nothing. The last three posts took four hours each. None of them generated a single lead. The agent has no idea why some pieces work and others don't.

Ad spend is a black box. $2,500 a month to an ad agency. The agent can't connect a single closing back to a single ad. Neither can the agency.

A competitor is winning listings the agent used to get. The agent doesn't know what the competitor is posting, where they're spending, or which neighborhoods they're targeting.

Postcards go out. Silence comes back. $1,200 on direct mail last quarter. Two phone calls. No idea which addresses scanned the QR code and which ones threw the card in the recycling.

Every closed deal should compound. Most don't. The agent means to call back at six months, at the one-year anniversary, at the listing-decision moment. They forget. Past clients become other agents' new clients.

The site doesn't have to use all seven — pick the three or four that hit hardest. Whichever ones make the page, the discipline is the same: the agent should read them and think "yes, that's me" before they finish the second sentence.

"The thesis: any agent can buy the same tools. Almost no agent has the operating system to use them together. We are building the operating system."

02. The system in one diagram

Where PropCast fits in the bigger build

PropertyIQ is the platform. PropCast is the module we ship first, because content production is the top of the funnel and nothing downstream works until content is being made. PropReach amplifies what PropCast proves. PropFlow runs the CRM and lead lifecycle. PropClose runs the transaction. PropSearch underwrites investments. And Wattson is the AI agent layer that threads through every module, doing work the human agent would otherwise have to do.

You only have to build for PropCast right now. But the website's story has to make clear that this is module one of a platform, so the design language can absorb the next modules without being rebuilt from scratch.

03. Brand direction

Starting points, not specifications
Important framing: everything in this section is a starting point Claude (the AI that drafted this brief) generated, not something I'm personally invested in. The colors, the type, the motion — treat them as Claude's first pass that you should push back on, replace, or refine based on your own judgment. The principles below the spec table are what I actually believe. The specifics are negotiable.

The visual challenge is specific: PropertyIQ has to feel technologically serious without feeling cold, cutting-edge without feeling like a memecoin, and trustworthy without feeling like a 1990s mortgage broker. The audience is a licensed real estate professional who needs to put their reputation on the line by using this product. The brand has to earn that — and it has to combine four things at once:

  • Trust. Agents are putting their license and their clients in our hands.
  • Real estate. Without falling into the photo-of-a-suburban-house cliché.
  • Intelligence. The product is intelligence about a market. The brand should feel that way too.
  • Technology. Forward, edged, designed-by-people-with-opinions. Not safe SaaS.

What we want it to feel like

  • Operating system, not app. Closer to a Bloomberg Terminal redesigned by Linear than to a typical real estate website
  • Confident dark, not cold dark. A deep ink palette with a single warm accent. Negative space used deliberately, not because we have nothing to say
  • Editorial type. A serif or display sans for headlines paired with Inter (or similar) for body. Designed-not-default
  • Motion that respects the user's time. Eased, short, intentional. No "creative agency" wow animations that delay the read

What we want it to not feel like

  • Generic SaaS — Stripe-clone with gradient hero, three feature cards, testimonial carousel
  • Crypto / Web3 — neon gradients, holographic mesh, glassmorphism overdone
  • Traditional realtor — stock photos of houses, "your trusted partner" copy
  • AI startup of 2023 — purple gradient, sparkle icon, "powered by GPT" badge

04. Typography & the brand-storytelling concept

Where conceptual logos like BEAR, SHARK, and FIZZ come in

The brand should tell its story in the letters themselves wherever possible. Graeham loves a specific family of logo design — where the wordmark is the brand concept rather than a wordmark sitting next to an icon. I'm attaching three reference logos in the email with this brief:

BEAR

The bear is literally drawn into the letterforms of the word BEAR. You don't see a separate icon and wordmark — you see one shape that is simultaneously the word and the animal. The brand is the typography.

SHARK

The "S" is a shark, mid-swim, between two larger shark silhouettes. Again — the wordmark and the brand idea are the same object. You can't separate them.

FIZZ

The letters of FIZZ contain the fizz. Bubbles inside the letterforms, liquid filling them, a meniscus halfway up each character. The wordmark doesn't describe the product. It is the product.

That's the level of conceptual integration we want for PropertyIQ. Not necessarily a literal house inside the letters — please no — but some idea that connects intelligence + real estate + technology directly into the wordmark. Maybe a subtle data-grid texture inside the characters. Maybe a geographic-coordinate sliver. Maybe the dot of an "i" is a pin on a map. Maybe nothing visual at all and the typography itself just feels like infrastructure. Bring options.

Where to look for the typeface

Graeham has spent serious time on fontshare.com and myfonts.com and is open to suggestions. The bar is: the typeface should look intelligent, real-estate-credible, and technology-forward — and it should be a little bit cool and sexy without trying too hard. Mono-display hybrids, modernist serifs with computational character, geometric sans with editorial proportions — all viable. Recommend three to five typefaces with reasoning for each, and we'll pick.

05. The 3D / motion question

Telling the story with the visual

Graeham wants the marketing site to feel like a 3D-aware site, not a flat document with text on it. Not because 3D is currently trendy — because the best examples on the web right now use the 3D visual to tell the story, and the story and the visual become the same thing. Hover-and-rotate-a-3D-product-for-fun is not the goal. Visual-and-narrative-as-one-object is the goal.

Reference sites we want you to study

All of these are from the motionsites.ai showcase. Spend a few minutes on each.

Urban Jungle (positive ★)
motionsites.ai → Urban Jungle

This is the model. The 3D animation walks you through the brand's story as you scroll — urban transition early, then a 3D image pops up that visually is what the product does. Story and visual are inseparable.

WISA Space (positive ★)
motionsites.ai → WISA Space

Tells the story through the visual the same way Urban Jungle does. Scroll moves you through a coherent narrative; the 3D and the copy reinforce each other.

Synapsis Dark (negative ✗)
motionsites.ai → Synapsis Dark

Cool-looking site. But the imagery doesn't tell us what the company actually does. The visual is decoration, not narrative. Don't do this version.

"Your email revitalized" (negative ✗)
motionsites.ai → email landing page

A decorative ribbon flies around in the background. Looks fine. Says nothing about the product. This is the trap we want to avoid.

★

The ideal: the 3D tells the PropertyIQ story. The hero shows a fragment of a Bay Area neighborhood from above; as the visitor scrolls, content publishes from one of the homes, an ad reaches another, a household lights up as a raised-hand signal, a closing fires, and the camera pulls back to reveal the entire farm covered in signal. We're showing the loop, not describing it. That's a hard build — if you can't pull it off, abstract geometric 3D as a hero accent is acceptable. But the ideal is the visual is the story.

Do

  • Use a single 3D moment, big, and earn it — Urban Jungle / WISA Space pattern
  • Tie the 3D to the actual operating logic of PropertyIQ — neighborhood, attribution flow, command center surface
  • Use geometric / abstract 3D as accent in other sections — restrained, never decorative
  • Show real data in mockups — never lorem ipsum or placeholder numbers

Don't

  • Stock photos of houses, handshakes, "smiling realtor at laptop"
  • Floating 3D house icons. Anything literal-3D-real-estate is a trap
  • Mesh gradient blobs in the background
  • 3D for the sake of 3D when it doesn't carry meaning
  • Three-column feature card grid as the only landing-page asset

06. The PropCast landing page

Story architecture, not feature list

The landing page is the only chance to convert a skeptical real estate agent who has seen a hundred tools. The story has to land in under sixty seconds of scrolling. The structure below is the spine — your job is to make each section feel inevitable, like the next thing the visitor was already going to ask.

Landing page — section flow

The hero headline — multiple options to pressure-test

The previous draft of this brief had the headline as "The operating system real estate agents have been missing." Graeham's verdict: too generic, too small. The hero has to actually say what the system is and does, not just gesture at a category. Below are stronger options — pick the one you think lands hardest, or use them as inputs to your own. Sub-headline directly underneath should fill in the specific operational promise.

An operating system for real estate. One screen replaces seven.

Anchor: the consolidation argument. Direct, declarative. Sub-headline picks up the "what does it do" piece.

PropertyIQ watches the market, makes the content, runs the ads, finds the next seller — on one screen, with one AI doing the work in between.

Anchor: the full operating loop in one sentence. Long for a hero but specific. Earns its length by removing all mystery in 3 seconds.

Stop running seven tools. Run the system that runs the tools.

Anchor: the pain-point pivot. Shorter, sharper. Sub-headline names the specific tools and what PropertyIQ does in their place.

The intelligence layer for real estate. Built for the agent who runs their business like a CEO.

Anchor: position + audience identity. Sub-headline names what the layer does — generates content, attributes leads, predicts sellers, runs voice.

Your market, watched. Your content, made. Your leads, surfaced. Your ads, attributed. One system. One AI. One screen.

Anchor: Stripe-style cadence list. Editorial weight without being clever. Specific about each verb.

The right headline isn't a single sentence stitched together from feature names. It says what the agent gets and why this is different from everything else they've paid for.

Section-by-section direction

01 · Hero. Pick one of the headline options above (or write a better one). Sub-headline directly underneath delivers the specific operational promise. One primary CTA — "Talk to Wattson" — which opens the onboarding chat. The 3D backdrop is where we hope to make the Urban Jungle / WISA Space move: a fragment of a neighborhood, a content piece publishing, a household lighting up. If the full 3D narrative isn't possible, an abstract geometric form reacting to cursor is the fallback.

02 · The problem you already know. Three or four of the seven problems listed in Section 01 of this brief — paraphrased into the agent's own voice. Monday morning. Forty leads in the CRM. The competitor winning listings the agent used to get. Pure emotional resonance, no statistics.

03 · The single screen that replaces seven dashboards. The hero of the page, more than the hero. An animated, interactive mockup of the Intelligence Command Center. The user should see the heatmap pulse, a "raised hand" signal surface, a content recommendation appear. This is where we earn belief.

04 · How it actually works — the loop. One SVG flowchart animating as the visitor scrolls. Signal in → content out → distribution → capture → attribution → smarter recommendation next week. Six seconds total. Honest about what runs automatically and what requires the agent's approval.

05 · Proof. Side-by-side comparison. Left column: the agent who does this manually, with realistic time and cost estimate. Right column: the agent on PropCast, with the actual time and cost. Below the comparison, embedded video samples — a HeyGen avatar piece, a B-roll cutaway from Higgsfield, a market update with motion graphics. Real outputs, not stock previews.

06 · The CTA. Not "request a demo." Not "start your free trial." The CTA opens the Wattson onboarding chat directly. "Start a conversation with Wattson — five minutes to see what your market is doing right now." The conversation itself does the qualifying.

!

Critical: the landing page sells PropCast as the front door to PropertyIQ. Somewhere between sections 04 and 05 there should be a quiet section — "This is one module. Here is the system." — with a compact version of the architecture diagram from Section 02. Two sentences and the diagram. We're showing the visitor that the building is bigger than the door they are walking through.

07. Onboarding

A conversation, not a form — with two different paths

Every other SaaS in this space onboards with a wizard — six pages of dropdowns, integration toggles, and "tell us about your business" checkboxes. We are going to do the opposite. The agent's first interaction with the product is a conversation with Wattson, the AI agent that will be working for them after they sign up. The medium is the message: if Wattson can't even handle a conversation, how can it handle the agent's content?

The two paths through onboarding

The hardest design problem inside onboarding is that we have two very different new users, and the experience has to respect both:

New agent — high friction

A new agent has no sphere of influence yet, no existing HeyGen avatars, no ElevenLabs voice clone, no past-client list, no ad accounts to connect. Everything has to be created from zero. This is the friction point we have to make easy.

The fix: an in-app recording flow. PropertyIQ shows the agent a teleprompter script on screen, the agent records two clips of about three minutes each, and the system creates two starting avatars + a voice clone from those clips. No external tools. No "go set up an account on HeyGen first." Just open the laptop and record.

Experienced agent — low friction

An experienced agent already has avatars they like, a voice clone they've paid for, a past-client list in their CRM, and ad accounts running. Forcing them to re-record everything from scratch is insulting.

The fix: an import flow. OAuth into HeyGen and pull existing avatars. OAuth into ElevenLabs and pull existing voices. OAuth into GHL (or Follow Up Boss, or whatever CRM) and pull contacts. OAuth into Meta + Google + YouTube and inherit ad performance history. The system makes the connections, the agent doesn't reproduce them.

Open design question for you: should the avatar capture happen during the initial onboarding chat — as a hard gate before the agent can finish signup — or should it happen after the agent completes the conversational onboarding and lands in the Command Center, with the recording surfaced as the first prominent call-to-action? Graeham is genuinely undecided. The gate is more committal (forces the work to happen) but might scare off agents who aren't ready to film themselves on the spot. The post-onboarding placement is gentler but creates an empty-state problem where the agent lands on a Command Center they can't fully use. Bring your opinion to the next session.
Wattson-driven onboarding flow — both paths

The avatar capture deserves its own screen

The hardest moment in onboarding (for new agents) is asking them to point a camera at their face for six total minutes. We don't let the user skip it, but we have to make it feel professional, not invasive. The capture step is the one screen where the design should slow down and feel cinematic — full bleed, dim ambient UI, an on-screen teleprompter with the script Wattson generated based on the agent's market, a quiet pulsing record indicator, and a clear "you'll need this for the system to ship content for you" framing.

Behind the scenes the system is doing two things with those clips. Clip one trains the HeyGen avatar — the agent's video twin used in every market update and explainer. Clip two extracts audio and sends it to ElevenLabs to clone the agent's voice — used in voice calls, voiceovers, and any audio-only artifact. The user doesn't need to know the technical split; they just need to record cleanly.

★

Avatar atrophy = progressive onboarding, not a separate concept. The reason the agent only records two starting clips at signup (not fifty) is that we are spreading the recording workload across the year. The system tracks how often each avatar look gets used. When one approaches over-use or inactivity, the agent gets a notification — first a soft one ("your avatar is approaching fatigue, consider recording a new look"), then a firmer one ("your avatar is at fatigue — record a new one to keep your content fresh"). The agent records a new clip directly in-app, the system rotates the tired look out of production for a while, and over the course of the year the agent organically ends up with five to seven looks. That's progressive onboarding, not a single intimidating up-front ask.

08. The Intelligence Command Center

Where the agent actually lives

This is the hardest design problem in the whole product. The Command Center has to surface a meaningful amount of information without overwhelming the user. The solution is layering — at default depth, the screen is calm. At one click into Intelligence Mode, every panel reveals the data and reasoning behind what it is showing. The user controls the depth.

What "Intelligence Mode" actually means: it's the toggle that shows the agent the in-depth process the system used to arrive at the answer it gave them — but visually, smoothly, and easily. Complexity is still there underneath; the toggle makes it visible without making it overwhelming. Think of it as the difference between a chess engine showing you its move (Mode OFF) versus the same engine showing you its top three candidate moves, the evaluation scores, and the lines it considered (Mode ON). Same engine. Different depth on screen.
