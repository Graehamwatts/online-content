01. Why this matters

The agent's real problem

Real estate agents are drowning in tools and starving for outcomes. A typical top-25% agent today is paying for a CRM, an MLS subscription, a transaction management platform, a postcard print service, an ad agency, a video editor, a virtual assistant, a coaching subscription, and three or four social schedulers. None of these tools talk to each other. None of them tell the agent what to do next. Every Monday, the agent stares at a screen full of dashboards and still has no idea which lead matters most, which content piece deserves spend, or which household in their farm is closest to selling.

PropertyIQ replaces the dashboard problem with a command center. Instead of seven tools and zero answers, the agent opens one screen that tells them: here is what your market did this week, here is what we recommend you post, here is the household most likely to call you back, here is what your ads earned you. The complexity is still there — it has to be, because the work is genuinely complex — but the surface is calm. The depth is one click away when the agent wants it.

"The thesis: any agent can buy the same tools. Almost no agent has the operating system to use them together. We are building the operating system."

That positioning is what the website has to communicate. Not "another SaaS for realtors." The agent has seen forty of those. What they have never seen is a single system that takes them from I have no content this week to three pieces are live, twelve leads came in, two are likely sellers, here is your call list — without them touching a dozen products to make it happen.

02. The system in one diagram

Where PropCast fits in the bigger build

PropertyIQ is the platform. PropCast is the module we ship first, because content production is the top of the funnel and nothing downstream works until content is being made. PropReach amplifies what PropCast proves. PropFlow runs the CRM and lead lifecycle. PropClose runs the transaction. PropSearch underwrites investments. And Wattson is the AI agent layer that threads through every module, doing work the human agent would otherwise have to do.

You only have to build for PropCast right now. But the website's story has to make clear that this is module one of a platform, so the design language can absorb the next modules without being rebuilt from scratch.

03. Brand mood

Trust, intelligence, edge — not crypto

The visual challenge is specific: PropertyIQ has to feel technologically serious without feeling cold, cutting-edge without feeling like a memecoin, and trustworthy without feeling like a 1990s mortgage broker. The audience is a licensed real estate professional who needs to put their reputation on the line by using this product. The brand has to earn that.

What we want it to feel like

  • Operating system, not app. Visual language closer to a Bloomberg Terminal redesigned by Linear than a typical real estate website
  • Confident dark, not cold dark. Deep navy and warm off-white, with gold as the only accent. Negative space used deliberately, not because we have nothing to say
  • Editorial type, not generic SaaS. A serif or display sans (Syne, Söhne, GT Sectra) for headlines paired with Inter for body
  • 3D used as accent, not as everything. A subtle WebGL element behind the hero (geometric, abstract — not floating houses). Maybe a 3D map of a Bay Area neighborhood reacting to scroll. Restraint is the whole game
  • Motion that respects the user's time. Eased, short, intentional. No "creative agency" wow animations that delay the read

What we want it to not feel like

  • Generic SaaS Stripe-clone with a gradient hero, three feature cards, and a testimonial carousel
  • Crypto / Web3 — neon gradients, holographic mesh blobs, glassmorphism overdone, "decentralized real estate" energy
  • Traditional realtor — stock images of suburban houses, blue-and-gold rosettes, "your trusted partner" copy
  • AI startup of 2023 — purple gradient, sparkle icon, "powered by GPT-4" badge
  • Overdesigned. If the agent can't immediately tell what PropertyIQ does within five seconds of landing, the design failed

Do

  • Use a single accent color per page section, never three at once
  • Treat negative space like it's expensive — because it is
  • Use a subtle WebGL 3D element behind the hero (one, not many)
  • Show real data in mockups — never lorem ipsum or placeholder numbers
  • Build for the agent who is skeptical of AI hype

Don't

  • Floating 3D house icons. Anything 3D should be abstract / geometric
  • Sparkle icons next to the word "AI"
  • Mesh gradient blobs in the background
  • Three-column feature card grid as the only landing-page asset

04. The PropCast landing page

Story architecture, not feature list

The landing page is the only chance to convert a skeptical real estate agent who has seen a hundred tools. The story has to land in under sixty seconds of scrolling. The structure below is the spine — the designer's job is to make each section feel inevitable, like the next thing the visitor was already going to ask.

Landing page — section flow

Section-by-section direction

01 · Hero. One headline. Seven words or fewer. Has to answer "what is this" with zero ambiguity. Working candidate: "The operating system real estate agents have been missing." Below it, one supporting line. One primary CTA — "Talk to Wattson" — which opens the onboarding chat. Background uses a subtle 3D element — a slowly rotating wireframe map of a Bay Area neighborhood, or an abstract geometric form that reacts to cursor. Restraint is the point.

02 · The problem you already know. Three short paragraphs naming the pain. Monday morning. Seven tabs open. Forty leads in the CRM that you have not touched in two weeks. The agent should read this and recognize themselves in five seconds. No statistics here — pure emotional resonance.

03 · The single screen that replaces seven dashboards. The hero of the page, even more than the hero. An animated, interactive mockup of the Intelligence Command Center. The user should see the heatmap pulse, see a "raised hand" signal surface, see a content recommendation appear. This is where we earn the visitor's belief.

04 · How it actually works — the loop. One SVG flowchart that animates as the visitor scrolls. Signal in → content out → distribution → capture → attribution → smarter recommendation next week. Six seconds total. Honest about what runs automatically and what requires the agent's approval.

05 · Proof. A side-by-side comparison. Left column: "the agent who does this manually" with a realistic time and cost estimate. Right column: "the agent on PropCast" with the actual time and cost. Below the comparison, embedded video samples — a HeyGen avatar piece, a B-roll cutaway from Higgsfield, a market update with motion graphics. Real outputs, not stock previews.

06 · The CTA. Not "request a demo." Not "start your free trial." The CTA opens the Wattson onboarding chat directly. "Start a conversation with Wattson — five minutes to see what your market is doing right now." The conversation itself does the qualifying.

!

Critical: the landing page is not selling PropCast in isolation. It is selling PropCast as the front door to PropertyIQ. Somewhere on the page — probably between sections 04 and 05 — there should be a quiet section titled something like "This is one module. Here is the system." with a compact version of the architecture diagram from Section 02 of this brief. Two sentences and the diagram. We are showing the visitor that the building is bigger than the door they are walking through.

05. Onboarding

A conversation, not a form

Every other SaaS in this space onboards with a wizard — six pages of dropdowns, integration toggles, and "tell us about your business" checkboxes. We are going to do the opposite. The agent's first interaction with the product is a conversation with Wattson, the AI agent that will be working for them after they sign up. The medium is the message: if Wattson can't even handle a conversation, how can it handle their content?

The onboarding flow does five things, and the user only ever sees one of them at a time:

Wattson-driven onboarding flow

The avatar capture deserves its own design treatment

The hardest moment in the entire onboarding is asking the agent to point a camera at their face for six total minutes. We do not let the user skip it, but we have to make it feel professional, not invasive. The capture step is the one screen in the entire flow where the design should slow down and feel cinematic — full bleed, dim ambient lighting in the UI, an on-screen teleprompter with the script Wattson generated based on the agent's market, a quiet pulsing record indicator, and a clear "you'll need this for the system to ship content for you" framing.

Behind the scenes, the system is doing two things with those clips. Clip one trains the HeyGen avatar — the agent's video twin used in every market update and explainer. Clip two extracts audio and sends it to ElevenLabs to clone the agent's voice — used in voice calls, voiceovers, and any audio-only artifact. The user does not need to know the technical split; they just need to record cleanly.

★

Avatar atrophy is a design problem that will surface inside the Command Center. The agent only records two starting clips at signup, but the system tracks how often each avatar look has been used. When a look approaches over-use (12+ pieces) or inactivity (90+ days), the system surfaces a one-tap "refresh your avatar" prompt and the production queue routes around the tired look. By month six the agent will have five to seven avatar looks naturally — never recorded all at once.

06. The Intelligence Command Center

Where the agent actually lives

This is the hardest design problem in the whole product. The Command Center has to surface a meaningful amount of information without overwhelming the user. The solution is layering — at default depth, the screen is calm. At one click into Intelligence Mode, every panel reveals the data and reasoning behind what it is showing. The user controls the depth.

The Command Center is also what the marketing site has to show off to convert visitors. The landing-page mockup is essentially a stripped-down preview of the real thing. Design the real thing first; the marketing mockup is downstream of it.

The discipline of Intelligence Mode

Intelligence Mode is the single most important interaction in the entire app. When OFF (default for new users), every card on the Command Center shows the answer — the recommended content, the lead to call, the metric. When ON (a global toggle in the header), every card grows a footer panel that reveals the reasoning: what signals drove the recommendation, what competing options were rejected, what data charts support the call, what outcome the system expects.

The agent toggles the mode based on how much depth they want at that moment. A power user might leave it on permanently. A newer agent might only flip it on when they want to learn what the system is doing. Either way, the depth is one click away and never forced.

Six things the Command Center must do at default depth

  1. Show today's raised-hand signals at the top, with the address and the reason it raised its hand
  2. Show the heatmap as the hero of the screen — every household, every touch, visible spatially
  3. Show this week's content queue with one-click approve / edit / reschedule
  4. Show the attribution number that matters — quarter-to-date pipeline value attributed to content
  5. Show the avatar strip — how many looks the agent has, which are fresh, which are aging
  6. Surface the next best action the agent can take in the next ten minutes

Heatmap — the hardest single component to design well

The heatmap is doing more work than a heatmap usually does. It is not just showing density of touches. It is the spatial representation of the agent's entire marketing presence in their farm. Each household has its own record. Click any household and you see: how many organic content impressions reached this address, how many paid ad impressions, how many postcards were delivered, whether the QR was scanned, whether anyone in the household engaged with a saved listing, whether a raised-hand signal has fired in the last 30 days. The map is the agent's daily work surface, and the rest of the dashboard is supporting it.

For the website mockup, the heatmap does not need to be functional — but it has to feel like it could be. Glowing pins of varying brightness across an abstract Bay Area neighborhood grid. One or two pulsing pins indicating raised-hand signals. A tooltip on hover showing what is happening at that address. That single component on the landing page does more conversion work than any feature card grid.

07. What's in the GitHub repo

And what to improve

The repo at github.com/Graehamwatts/propiq-ui contains early-iteration sketches of the PropertyIQ UI. They were built quickly to get ideas out of my head and onto a screen — not to set the design direction. You should treat them as raw input, not as a starting template to refine.

What's worth pulling forward from the repo:

  • The information architecture instinct — the way modules and sub-tools relate to each other
  • The basic content categorization — content buckets, attribution loops, the underlying data model
  • The mental model of "Command Center" as a single entry point

What to throw away:

  • The current visual treatment — every page in the repo looks like a generic SaaS dashboard. None of it carries the brand mood described in Section 03 of this brief
  • The layout density — too dense, too many elements competing for attention, no clear hierarchy
  • The current typography — system font stack, no editorial weight, no personality
  • The lack of motion design — everything is static. The product should feel like it's alive in subtle, controlled ways

Your real reference point is this brief and the three reference moods below — not the repo. The repo confirms what needs to be built. This brief defines how it should look and feel.
