The Investigation. Square Footage, Bathrooms & HOA

Square footage. You've told me the unit measures 1,580 sq ft. Two independent public-record sources say otherwise and agree with each other exactly: the San Mateo County Assessor's property characteristics (via Orange Coast Title's property profile) list 1,510 sq ft, and the CoreLogic tax record, pulled separately, also lists Building Sq Ft at 1,510 with the same figure repeated in its Realist-sourced "Primary SqFt" field. That second figure is the one your own active MLS listing already uses. Its $/sq ft field on the listing is $601.91, and $908,888 divided by 1,510 comes out to $601.91 almost to the penny, confirming the listing itself is already built on the 1,510 number, not 1,580.

I could not find a documentary basis anywhere in the county or title record for 1,580. It's possible your 1,580 number includes an enclosed patio, a stairwell or landing counted differently, or simply reflects a builder's brochure figure from 1980 that was never reconciled with the assessor's measurement, which happens often in older HOA-era condo developments. Here's what I'd recommend: keep the county-verified 1,510 as the primary listed square footage, since that's what title, tax, and the MLS field all already agree on, and add a square footage disclosure noting that you believe the unit measures 1,580 sq ft, that the county assessor's record shows 1,510 sq ft, and that buyers should verify square footage to their own satisfaction, ideally with their own measurement, before removing contingencies. That's standard California practice for exactly this kind of gap and protects both of us going into escrow.

Bedrooms and bathrooms. The tax record actually already supports your 2.5-bath description; it was likely just read wrong. The county field is formatted "Baths (F/H): 2/1," meaning 2 full baths and 1 half bath, which is 2.5 baths, the same as what is marketed. There is no real discrepancy here once the format is understood, and every other unit at Mission Palo Alto in the same title package shows the identical 2/1 configuration, which confirms 2.5 baths is the standard floor plan for this complex, not an anomaly for this unit.

HOA fee. This is the one that needs a phone call before it goes any further. The current MLS listing shows $460 per year, which is not a plausible HOA fee for a 51-unit gated complex with a community pool, spa, security gates, and exterior maintenance. A quick check against another Mission Palo Alto unit's public listing data shows a fee closer to $410 per month for the same community. My read is that $460 was very likely entered as an annual figure when it should be monthly, or the field simply carried over incorrectly from a prior listing cycle. This needs to be confirmed directly with the HOA management company for Mission Palo Alto, Ferrari Community Management, before another buyer's lender or agent catches the gap during due diligence. Getting this exact number is important since it affects a buyer's real monthly cost and their loan qualification math, and an incorrect number discovered mid-escrow is a credibility problem you do not want.

Where the Market Is. Rates and the Single-Family vs. Condo Split

The 30-year fixed rate sits at 6.75% today, up from 6.59% a week ago, a move of roughly 0.16 points that puts rates near their highest point in the past year. That is the direct answer to the rate question this week: yes, rates moved up, and yes, the size of the move (about a tenth of a point in a single week) is enough to matter to a retail buyer's monthly payment and their approval amount, even if it does not change much for an all-cash or highly leveraged investor buyer.

The more interesting story this week is the split between single-family homes and attached product, and it runs in different directions depending on which market you are standing in. In San Francisco proper, single-family homes are up 22.6% year over year to a $2.2M median, selling in 12 days at close to 25% over asking, driven by tight inventory (down nearly 45% year over year) and a buyer pool with real cash from tech and AI-sector wealth. San Francisco condos have also turned, now selling in 16 days at roughly 7% over asking, a sharp reversal from the buyer's market condition condos were in a year ago; the same AI-hiring wave, return-to-office mandates, and high rents are pulling buyers back into condos there specifically.

That recovery is a San Francisco micro-market story, not a Peninsula one. Across San Mateo County broadly, condo and townhome inventory has nearly doubled year over year (239 active listings this June versus 117 in June 2023) while the number of units actually going into contract has stayed flat, and county-wide condo median pricing is down roughly 8% year over year. Days on market for condos and townhomes in the county have been running in the 44 to 55 day range, more than triple San Francisco's condo pace. Single-family homes in East Palo Alto specifically are still moving at a healthier clip, a 27-day median with a 103% sale-to-list ratio in the second quarter, but that single-family strength has not pulled the local condo and townhome tier along with it the way it has in San Francisco.

Put together: your home is an attached product in a submarket that is absorbing more supply and taking longer to sell than either the SF condo boom or the local single-family tier, at the same time rates just ticked up for the second week running. None of that is a reason to panic on price, since the same-complex evidence below is genuinely strong, but it argues against pushing for a stretch number right now and for staying priced where the actual comp data points, which is what the recommendation below does.

The Market Story

The strongest evidence for value here comes from inside your own complex. Two Mission Palo Alto units, both smaller than yours at 1,280 sq ft against your 1,510, sold within the last 15 months at $960,000 and $1,030,000. Those are real closed transactions in the identical community, five and six hundredths of a mile away, and they set a real floor for what this complex can command. At the same time, per-square-foot math does not translate cleanly upward just because your unit is bigger; buyers pay for the home they walk into, not a multiplication table, so those numbers support a strong result without justifying stretching the price simply because your unit has more square footage.

The next ring out tells a more moderate story. The Woodland Creek condos on West Bayshore, a comparable 2002-built community about four-tenths of a mile away, sold in the $760,000 to $775,000 range over the past year at $639 to $647 per square foot. The O'Keefe Townhouses just across the Palo Alto line, similar in age and configuration with 1.5 baths instead of 2.5, sold in the $712,500 to $765,000 range at $603 to $647 per square foot. Both of those cohorts anchor a more conservative reading of the market than the two same-complex sales alone would suggest, and both matter because they represent what an East Palo Alto or immediately-adjacent attached-home buyer is actually paying today, not what a single strong outlier suggests is possible.

Worth correcting for the record: an earlier review of this property referenced a "sister unit" that reportedly went into contract at $924,888 in 20 days this spring. Working back through the property's own MLS history, that record is actually this unit's own first 2026 listing attempt, launched 4/14/2026 by the prior brokerage at $924,888 and withdrawn 20 days later on 5/4/2026, not sold and not put under contract. It was relisted at $907,880 in May, withdrawn again in June, then relaunched under your current listing on 7/8/2026 at $908,888. That is a materially different story than a fast sale at a higher price, and it matters here because it means the property has been market-tested twice this year without a buyer, at prices at and above where it sits today, before any of this week's showing feedback or rate movement.

Taken together with the feedback from four separate showings this cycle pointing at area, location, and price rather than the unit itself, the honest read is that the same-complex sales prove the ceiling is real, the broader ring of comps sets a more grounded floor and middle, and the market has already shown twice this year that pushing toward the top of that range without a buyer already in hand has not worked. That argues for pricing into the middle of the supported range rather than testing the top of it again.

Pricing Strategy Analysis

Three approaches, with the actual data behind each:

Price Below Market

A launch in the $875,000 to $895,000 range, just under the Woodland Creek and O'Keefe comp cluster, would likely generate fast interest and a quick contract, trading some upside for speed and certainty. Worth serious consideration given the county-wide condo DOM trend and the fact that this listing has already sat unsold twice this year at higher numbers.

Price At Market

The current $908,888 sits almost exactly at the midpoint of the two supported comp tiers, above the Woodland Creek/O'Keefe cluster and meaningfully below the two same-complex sales. This is a defensible, data-supported number, not an overpriced one, and the honest read is that the showing feedback pointing at "price" is more likely pointing at the broader softening condo market and this weekend's environmental friction than at this specific number being wrong.

Price Above Market

Testing toward $950,000 or higher would lean on the two same-complex sales alone. Given that this property has already tested the $907,880 to $924,888 range twice this year without a buyer, and given the rate and inventory headwinds documented above, a third attempt at a higher number carries real risk of extending days on market rather than capturing a better price.

Recommended List Price. An Independent Read, Not Anchored to the Current List

Setting aside what is already on the MLS and starting fresh from the comp data alone: the two same-complex sales at $960,000 and $1,030,000 set a real ceiling, the Woodland Creek and O'Keefe cohort at $712,500 to $775,000 sets a real floor, and the honest midpoint of those two tiers, adjusted down modestly for the current rate environment and the county-wide condo softening, lands close to where the property is already priced.

Square footage disclosure

List at the county-verified 1,510 sq ft, since that already matches title, tax, and your own MLS $/sq ft field, and add a square footage disclosure noting your stated 1,580 sq ft alongside the assessor's 1,510 sq ft figure, with a note that buyers should independently verify square footage before removing contingencies. HOA fee correction

Confirm the actual fee directly with Ferrari Community Management before the next showing cycle. The $460 annual figure currently on the MLS is very likely a data entry error, plausibly a monthly figure entered as annual, and a buyer's lender will catch this during underwriting if it is not corrected first.

Perception and security at the gate

This week's two incidents, the taco vendor gathering and the unidentified person at the gate, are both addressable through a short conversation with the HOA about weekend activity and access control rather than anything to do with pricing or the unit itself. Worth resolving before the next open house since first impressions at the gate carry outsized weight for a gated-community buyer.
