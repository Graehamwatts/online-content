Over the trailing 12 months, exactly 8 condos sold citywide, and 7 of those 8 were inside this building. There is no meaningful outside comp set to lean on here. Your own building is the market.

Since July, the two closest possible comps to your unit have both closed. #223, the identical 776 sq ft floor plan, sold for $580,000 on July 29, the sale I personally handled. #310, a slightly larger 821 sq ft unit, sold for $560,000 on July 28, about 2.6% under its asking price despite going pending in under a week. The more telling story is #322, the other identical floor plan in the building, still on the market right now. Reading the numbers

Of the five Woodland Creek 1-bedroom transactions with real numbers behind them over the last 15 months:

  • #223 sold at $580,000 three weeks ago, the identical floor plan to yours, no original list to compare since it was Graeham's own listing throughout, but the closing number itself is the strongest single data point in this report.
  • #115 and #230 sold slightly over their original ask (103.4% and 101.3%), both in under three weeks, back in mid-2024.
  • #310 sold under its original ask (97.4%) this past July, despite going pending in just 6 days. Fast does not guarantee over-ask in this building anymore.
  • #332 overpriced badly and paid for it: listed $595,000, sat 235+ days, sold for $525,000, 88.2% of its original ask.
  • #322 is living that same story right now. Listed $595,000, cut to $575,000, cut again to $549,000, still unsold after 95 days.
The pattern holds across both cycles: units that opened near what the building actually supports sold within three weeks. Units that opened above it sat for months and gave the money back anyway.
Why is the market pricing this way? This is a 65-unit building with roughly one 1-bedroom sale every six months. There is no depth of buyer demand to absorb an aspirational number the way a larger, more liquid market might. A buyer shopping this floor plan has exactly one or two real alternatives at any given time, so if your unit opens meaningfully above what the last comparable sale actually closed at, that buyer simply waits for #322 to cut again instead.
What this all means. The evidence argues for anchoring your list price close to what #223 actually proved the floor plan is worth, then letting your unit's better orientation carry it to the top of that range rather than starting above it. #322 is showing you, in real time, what happens to the alternative.

Special Considerations

Appraisal risk is real but better supported than in July. Most buyers for this unit will finance, and a low appraisal is the one thing that can cap a deal regardless of what a buyer is willing to pay. You now have two fresh, closed comps in the $560,000 to $580,000 range for an appraiser to work from, which is meaningfully stronger ground than the estimate-only picture from July. Staying inside the recommended range keeps you on the safe side of that risk.

#322 is your pricing benchmark, not just your competition. A buyer touring your unit will very likely have already seen or heard about #322's price history. Opening above where that unit currently sits, after two cuts, invites an immediate anchoring comparison you don't want. Opening at or near #223's proven number sidesteps it entirely.

No payoff to net against the sale. Your 2015 purchase was all cash, so there is no mortgage balance reducing your proceeds. That gives you real flexibility to price for a fast, clean sale without a minimum-net constraint forcing a higher number.
