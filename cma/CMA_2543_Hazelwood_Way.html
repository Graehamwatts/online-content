THE SUBJECT PROPERTY

2543 Hazelwood Way is a 3 bedroom, 2 bath home at 1,100 square feet on a 5,105 square foot lot, built in 1956. You and Janet have owned it since 2009. It is not currently owner-occupied.

The home needs a full renovation: kitchen, both bathrooms, flooring, and most of the major systems. Behind the garage there is an addition that opens up the kitchen and adds another room beyond that, functioning as extra living space. That addition is likely unpermitted. The street itself is solid, but the two homes directly across from the subject are not well kept, which is a real factor buyers will notice on a drive-by.

None of this is unusual for this specific block. The house immediately next door sold last year with its own disclosed unpermitted backyard structures, and the comp that sold twice below also carried an unpermitted square footage flag when it traded as a fixer. Buyers and lenders in this micro-market have shown they will still transact around that kind of disclosure.

ESTIMATED RENOVATION SCOPE AND COST

Rather than estimate this abstractly, the most reliable number available is what a contractor actually spent to renovate the closest matching comp. 2512 Farrington Way's disclosed renovation covered a new roof, new windows and doors, new furnace and ductwork, complete new electrical and copper plumbing, new flooring and insulation throughout, a fully remodeled kitchen with quartz counters and an island, and two remodeled bathrooms with new tile, vanities, and shower enclosures, plus a new deck, fencing, and landscaping.

That is essentially the same scope 2543 Hazelwood needs. On a 1956-built, 1,100 square foot home with this scope, a realistic all-in renovation budget in today's Bay Area market runs $185,000 to $240,000 for the core house. The garage-behind addition adds a separate line: finishing it properly, or pursuing a retroactive permit, is a further $15,000 to $30,000 depending on what a contractor finds once walls are opened up. Call it $200,000 to $270,000 all-in, and get two or three actual contractor bids before committing. This is a professional estimate, not a bid.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOUR DECISION

The edge is real, but thin
With the as-is value at $750,000 to $825,000, renovating nets an estimated $24,000 more than selling as-is in the base case. That is a real edge, but it is thin enough that a modest cost overrun or a slightly softer resale could erase it.
The contractor bid is now the swing factor
If the actual renovation cost comes in above roughly $234,000, selling as-is now nets about the same as renovating. Get two or three real bids before committing, not just the estimate here.
The permit path is where the real upside is
Getting the garage addition permitted moves the net advantage from about $24,000 to about $94,000. Worth finding out early how realistic that permit is, since it changes which path makes sense.
The unpermitted addition needs a plan, not a guess
Disclose it either way. Have a contractor and, if needed, the city weigh in on whether to finish it as bonus space as-is, pursue a retroactive permit, or scale it back, before the renovation budget is finalized.
