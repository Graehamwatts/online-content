The Bottom Line, Up Front

These two adjoining lots are worth far more as one combined redevelopment site than as two homes to fix and flip. The land, not the buildings, is the asset. Here is the short version of where the money is:

  • As-is, two ways to look at it: as two residential tear-downs, about $2.6M combined. But valued as commercial / mixed-use land, which is the right lens for a warehouse in an industrial zone, recent Redwood City commercial sales support roughly $3.5M to $4.25M for the combined half-acre. The land lens wins, because that is who actually buys this.
  • The renovate-and-flip idea (the ~$300K profit thesis): honestly, it does not pencil to $300K. The land value already sits inside the as-is price, so after Peninsula renovation costs the margin is closer to break-even. I walk through the math below.
  • Highest and best use: sell the combined half-acre to a builder for a mixed-use redevelopment. Under today's zoning that supports roughly 18–24 units; with a density bonus and the County's active rezoning, neighboring sites are getting 5 to 7 stories and 80–170 units. That entitlement upside is what gets to the top of the range.
  • My recommended target: price it at $3.5M as the realistic number, with $4.25M at the high end if the right developer competes for the density. I would not count on $5M. The raw commercial-land comps come in lower, and a comparable site down the road is sitting on the market unsold at a number in that range, which is the clearest sign the top is aspirational, not where it actually trades.
Why this is a developer's deal, not a flipper's deal. Combined, you have 22,791 sq ft (0.52 acre) in a mixed-use district that San Mateo County is actively upzoning. The two tired structures are a holding cost on the way to a much bigger building. A flipper pays for two renovated homes; a developer pays for half an acre of entitled-or-entitleable density. The developer number is higher.

What Can Actually Be Built Here

The site sits in unincorporated North Fair Oaks (a Redwood City mailing address), zoned M-1/NFO, the Mixed-Use Industrial / North Fair Oaks District. Permitting runs through San Mateo County, which is the more development-friendly path right now. The base development envelope:

The one real constraint to know. Straight apartment buildings are only allowed by-right within 200 ft of Fair Oaks Avenue. Warrington is just outside that line, so residential here has to come either as live/work units or as the residential portion of a mixed-use project (ground-floor commercial or work space, homes above), both by use permit. In practice that is exactly the product the corridor is building, so it is a process detail, not a dealbreaker.
Where the upside is. The County's North Fair Oaks Community Plan steers this industrial-transition pocket toward mixed-use redevelopment, and there is an active County rezoning and General Plan amendment underway to allow taller buildings and higher density. Right around the corner: 2949 Edison Way was proposed at 169 units and 7 stories, Monarch at Redwood broke ground at 86 units / 6 stories, and Middlefield Junction is 179 units. A developer who underwrites a density bonus on Warrington is underwriting that same trajectory, and that is what justifies a price above the as-is land value.

The leverage is the density bonus and the active County rezoning: doubling the unit count toward 40–60+ units (the direction Edison Way and the Middlefield corridor are already going) spreads the land cost across far more homes and pushes the residual land value to $3.5M–$5.0M+. Net: a developer who underwrites only base zoning pays around the as-is price (~$2.8M). A developer who underwrites the density-bonus upside, which the neighborhood is actively proving out, pays a premium. The job in the listing is to bring those developers to the table and make them compete.

My Recommendation

Jesse, here is how I would play it to get you the best price:

  • Sell the two parcels together, marketed as a commercial / mixed-use redevelopment site, to developers and builders, not as two fixers to a flipper. Price at $3.5M as the realistic target, with $4.25M at the high end if a developer competes. Do not chase $5M: a comparable site down the road is already sitting unsold near there.
  • Lead with the land story, priced as commercial: half an acre, mixed-use industrial zoning, in a corridor the County is actively upzoning, next to projects already approved at 6–7 stories. Recent commercial land in Redwood City traded at $159 to $262 per land foot, which is what supports the number and creates competition.
  • Don't spend money renovating first. The flip margin is thin and the renovation does not lift the land value. Keep the shop's month-to-month income in place (it's a small holding offset) and sell into the as-is condition.
  • Set a realistic floor: if a developer buyer does not materialize at the number, the parcels sell separately as a fixer house (~$1.2M) and a duplex-plus-shop income property (~$1.35M–$1.45M), about $2.55M–$2.65M combined. That is your downside, and it is still strong.
On the $300K flip: I would steer Jesse away from it. The land value is already in the as-is price, so the renovation margin is close to break-even and carries real execution risk. The bigger, cleaner number comes from selling the dirt to someone building the next 20-to-60 units, not from flipping two tired houses.
