A fair question you might ask about those two record sales: were they new like yours? No, and that is the point. 263 Medford was 59 years old when it sold, and 22711 Woodridge was 72 years old. Their prices did not come from condition, they came from land and sheer building size: both sat on roughly 18,000 sq ft lots (2.2 times yours), and Woodridge had over 5,000 sq ft of structure. That is what drives price in the income market. Your project's edge, brand-new everything, is real, and it is worth the most in front of the buyers who pay for condition: families buying a home to live in. That is exactly why the individual-sale path is where your newness gets paid for.

Rates matter to every scenario here. Inflation remains above the Fed's target, oil prices have added pressure since early this year, and the June Fed meeting turned hawkish, with most policymakers now expecting a rate increase rather than a cut, and markets pointing at late summer or September. For this project that cuts two ways: investor financing stays expensive (holding the Scenario A ceiling down), and retail buyers' payments stay stretched (explaining the long days-on-market on new construction). The strategic implication is simple: waiting for a better rate environment is not a plan this data supports. If anything, the window argues for getting the CO, starting the subdivision clock, and putting the first home on the market before financing gets more expensive, not less.
