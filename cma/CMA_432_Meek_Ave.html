Why Brand-New Doesn't Change The Ceiling More Than This

This is worth sitting with, because it is not intuitive. You spent real money building three brand-new homes, which feels like it should be worth more than a 60-year-old triplex. For a family buying one home to live in, it would be. For the investor pool that actually buys a one-parcel triplex, it mostly is not, and here is why.

New construction does add real value: no near-term roof, plumbing, or electrical surprises, a genuine selling point worth marketing hard. But that newness raises the price, and a higher price on the same rent lowers the cap rate, one of the top things investors compare deal to deal. At the top of the revised range, that cap rate lands around 3.9 percent, in line with what real Hayward triplexes and fourplexes are actually trading at right now, not thin by comparison (full detail in the cap rate section below).

Which buyer type wins out is not settled. Some pay up for low maintenance risk; others take the higher yield on an older building and self-insure the repair risk. What the sales record does confirm: a family buyer pays meaningfully more per door for one of these homes on its own than an investor pays for all three as a package (see the individual-home comps further down). That gap is why the individual-sale path is still worth several hundred thousand dollars more, and why, since the city's cost took that path off the table for now, the as-is ceiling lands where the comps below show it.
