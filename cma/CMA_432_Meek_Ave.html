Why Brand-New Doesn't Change The Ceiling More Than This

This is the part worth sitting with, because it is not intuitive. You spent real money building three brand-new homes, so it feels like that should be worth more than what a 60-year-old triplex would fetch. For a family buying a home to live in, it would be. For the buyer pool that actually buys a one-parcel triplex, an investor, it mostly is not, and here is exactly why.

An investor buying this as one income property is not paying for finishes. They are paying for the rent it produces, run through a cap rate, the same way every serious buyer in this segment underwrites a deal. Your homes are worth more to that buyer than an old triplex, but only through slightly higher achievable rent and lower near-term repair costs, not through a retail-style premium on the whole purchase price. That is the gap between what a family buyer would pay for one of these homes on its own (see the individual-home comps further down, they are meaningfully higher per door) and what an investor pays for all three together as a single asset. It is also exactly why the individual-sale path was worth $500K-plus more, and exactly why, now that path is off the table, the ceiling lands where it does.

Notice the trap built into the math: every dollar of extra price also raises the property tax, which lowers the income, which lowers the cap rate twice as fast. That is why the income ceiling is so firm, and why the two red rows are not a matter of finding a more optimistic investor. At 7 percent borrowing costs, a 4.3 percent cap loses money every month on financed dollars. The buyers who can ignore that math, families buying a home to live in with an owner-occupant mortgage, are exactly the buyers the individual-sale path puts in front of your three homes.

It's worth being direct about this, because it changes the "just wait it out" instinct. Coming into the year, the forecast was for rates to ease into the high-5s to low-6s by around now. That has not happened, rates have moved the other direction, and as of this week they just touched their highest point in more than a year. Inflation has stayed firmer, driven partly by higher gas prices, and broader geopolitical and trade uncertainty has kept upward pressure on the bond market that mortgage rates track. On top of that, we are heading out of summer and into the historically slower fall and winter selling season, on top of an already softer market outside of a few pockets like San Francisco proper.

For this project that cuts two ways: investor financing stays expensive, which is exactly what holds the Scenario A ceiling where it is, and retail buyers' payments stay stretched, which is what explains the long days-on-market on new construction townhomes even at strong locations. My honest prediction: I would not bank on a meaningfully better rate environment inside the next 6 to 9 months. It is possible things shift with time, but there is no fundamentals-based reason right now to expect it, and appreciation on this type of property is more likely to stay roughly flat than to climb and rescue a $2M-plus one-parcel price. Waiting for the market to come to your number is not a plan this data supports, whether you're waiting weeks or waiting through the winter. The number moves by doing the separation work, not by sitting on the market.
