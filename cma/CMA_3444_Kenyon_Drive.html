Reading the numbers

Of the 19 comparable Santa Clara homes that sold in the last six months:

  • 15 homes (79%) priced it to sell and sold over their original ask, a median of $2,450,000 (about 9% over asking), in a median of just 10 days.
  • Zero homes sold right at asking in this window, the market simply doesn't sit still at exactly 100%.
  • 4 homes (21%) overpriced and sold below their original ask. They started with a median list of $2,243,944 but sold for a median of $2,124,500, about 5% under, and took a median of 19 days, nearly double the priced-to-sell group.
  • Of the sales where I could confirm both the original and final list price, 1 of 7 (about 14%) needed at least one price cut before selling, 771 Pomeroy Avenue, which cut $75,000 and still closed under its reduced price.
The spread between those two groups is $325,500. Both started within $45,000 of each other on list price. Where they ended up was decided by how they were priced on day one.
Why is the market pricing this way? This looks like a straightforward anchoring gap. Most of the sellers in the "overpriced" bucket anchored to an optimistic number, a neighbor's headline sale, a Zestimate, the number they wanted to net, rather than what the actual comp set supported. The market corrected them over two to three weeks of soft activity, and they ultimately sold below where they started. The 79% majority who priced at or slightly under a defensible number got bid up fast instead. The correlation between price positioning and speed is real but modest (r = -0.23), the bigger reward for pricing right here isn't necessarily a faster sale, it's a better sale, the priced-to-sell group's median outcome is over $325,000 higher than the overpriced group's.
What this all means. The market punishes optimistic anchoring and rewards realistic, comp-backed pricing, not through speed alone but through final price. That is the whole case for the Recommended tier below, and it is why the list price I suggest sits under the expected sales price rather than on top of it. Listing in a range the closest verified comps actually support puts you in the 79% majority that gets bid up. Listing above it puts you in the 21% that cuts and settles under their own ask.

Special Considerations

The backyard is the one real project. The pool has been filled in and the jacuzzi removed, and that area does not currently read as usable yard. On a corner lot, where the private backyard is already closer to standard size than the 7,900 sq ft total implies, that space is doing real work in a buyer's mind. Rescaping it is the highest-return pre-list dollar you can spend, roughly $35,000 to $55,000, and it converts a question mark into a selling point: flat, low-maintenance yard with no pool upkeep, which actually widens your buyer pool to families who would have skipped a pool house entirely.

Lot size is an underused advantage. At 7,900 sq ft you are larger than most of the comps in this report, several of which sold in the $2.3M to $2.5M range on 5,500 to 6,400 sq ft lots. Corner position costs you some backyard privacy, but the total footprint is a genuine differentiator worth calling out directly in the marketing rather than leaving buyers to work out from the plat map.

Original hardwood, in good condition, is worth protecting. Buyers in this pocket pay for original oak that has not been carpeted over or replaced with LVT. Touch-up paint and new interior doors are small spends that let the floors carry the showing.
